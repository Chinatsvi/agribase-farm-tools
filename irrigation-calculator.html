💧 Crucial Notes Before You Irrigate

Read this first — good timing and technique save more water than any formula. Then scroll down to run the calculator.

⏰

Time it right

Irrigate early morning (5–9am) or late evening. Midday watering can lose 30%+ to evaporation and wind drift before it reaches the roots.

✋

Check the soil, not the calendar

Squeeze a handful of soil from root depth. If it holds shape and feels moist, hold off — irrigating on a fixed schedule regardless of soil moisture is the #1 cause of waterlogging.

🌾

Match the method to the crop

Drip suits high-value row crops (tomato, pepper, onion, banana). Sprinklers suit field crops and shallow-rooted vegetables. Flood/furrow only works well on level, well-drained ground.

🍂

Mulch to cut losses

A 5–7cm mulch layer (straw, grass, plastic) can reduce evaporation by 30–50%, meaning less water and fewer irrigation cycles for the same yield.

🌧️

Only credit "effective" rainfall

Heavy downpours mostly run off; light drizzle mostly evaporates. Only the rain that actually soaks into the root zone should offset your irrigation — don't count the full rain-gauge reading.

🧪

Know your water quality

Saline or hard borehole water builds up salts in the soil over time and can clog drip emitters. Test EC (electrical conductivity) before committing to large-scale drip irrigation.

🛑

Taper off before harvest

Stop or sharply reduce irrigation 1–3 weeks before harvest for onions, potatoes, grains and melons — this cures bulbs/tubers, boosts sugar content, and firms up fields for access.

⚠️

Efficiency numbers are real losses

The "system efficiency" in this calculator (e.g. 90% drip, 75% sprinkler, 55% furrow) isn't a formality — it's water that never reaches the crop. A poorly maintained system can lose far more than these baselines assume.

❓ Irrigation Questions & Answers

Common questions from farmers using this calculator.

What are ETo and ETc, and why do they matter?

ETo (reference evapotranspiration) is how much water a short, well-watered grass surface loses per day in your local weather — it's driven by sun, temperature, wind and humidity. ETc (crop evapotranspiration) is what your specific crop actually uses: ETc = ETo × Kc, where Kc is a crop coefficient that changes by growth stage. This calculator applies that FAO-56 formula for you.

Should I use Guided (FAO-56) mode or Manual Depth mode?

Use Guided mode if you don't know how many millimetres to apply — it works it out from your crop, growth stage and local weather. Use Manual Depth mode if you already have a target application depth (e.g. from an agronomist or irrigation design) and just want the litres/m³ for your field size.

How accurate is the GPS weather sync?

It pulls same-day ETo and rainfall estimates from Open-Meteo's forecast model for your location. It's a good working estimate for planning, but it's still a model, not a field measurement — cross-check with a rain gauge and local extension weather data for critical decisions, especially after unusual storms.

How do I know if I'm overwatering?

Warning signs include standing water after irrigation, yellowing lower leaves, soft or rotting roots, algae or moss on the soil surface, and a sour smell in the root zone. If soil stays wet more than a day after irrigation at root depth, extend your interval or reduce your application depth.

Which irrigation method gives the best water efficiency?

Drip/micro-drip is highest (around 90%) because water goes straight to the root zone with minimal evaporation or runoff. Furrow and basin flooding are lowest (55–65%) because much of the water is lost to deep percolation and runoff. Choose based on crop value, field slope, water availability and budget — not efficiency alone.

Can I calculate in Acres instead of Hectares?

Yes — both the Guided and Manual modes have an area-unit selector. Switch it to Acres and enter your field size; the calculator converts internally (1 acre = 0.404686 hectares) before working out litres and m³.

Why does the same crop show different water needs at different stages?

Water demand changes through a crop's life. Seedlings (Initial stage) need little water and are prone to rot if overwatered. Demand rises sharply during Vegetative growth, peaks at Mid-Season (flowering/fruiting — the most stress-sensitive stage for most crops), then usually drops in the Late-Season as the crop ripens and dries down.
